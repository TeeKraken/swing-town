Swing Town

A made-up town, two colors of voters, and six districts to draw. Slide a small swing in the vote and see which maps hold up.

Step 1 of 5

Meet Swing Town

    The number on each block is how many of its 100 voters vote orange. The rest vote purple. Thick lines are district borders.

    Pick a district, then tap or drag across blocks to paint them. On a keyboard: arrow keys move, Enter paints, keys 1 to 6 pick a district, Delete clears a block.

    The challenge

    Draw a map that keeps a majority of seats for orange (4 of 6) even after a 5-point swing toward purple.

    Need a hint?

    The greedy map wins big and loses big. The safe map wins less and holds. You need something in between. Let purple win two districts easily, and make each of your four districts at least 56% orange, so it's still over 50% after the swing.

      About this page

      Swing Town is made up. Orange and purple are just colors, not parties, and the town isn't a real place.

      The swing is a simplification. Here every block shifts by exactly the same amount. Real elections don't work that neatly, and this isn't a model of any real election. It's a way to see one idea: a map built on many thin wins can lose them all together.

      For the classroom. This is an unofficial companion to the gerrymandering puzzle from the Julia Robinson Mathematics Festival. It isn't made by or affiliated with JRMF. Their puzzle has students draw districts to help one color win; this page asks what happens to those maps when the vote moves a little.
